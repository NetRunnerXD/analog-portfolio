# Factor copy for the UI (F1–F13)

*Plain-language text for factor tooltips and cards, written for a general audience. Factor IDs and names match plan.md §2.2. Each entry has a short **label** and one or two sentences of **copy**. Numbers come from research.md, gaps.json and targets.json; check them again in the Sun 07:00 fact-check if any change. Researcher, 8 Oct 2026 (IST).*

---

### F1 · Mean surface temperature
**Label:** Average ground temperature
**Copy:** The average temperature of the ground over a full day and year. Cold limits how batteries, rovers and people work, and it controls whether water stays frozen. The Moon's shadowed polar craters are far colder than anywhere on Earth, so we match as closely as Earth allows and show what's left over.

### F2 · Day–night temperature swing
**Label:** Day–night temperature swing
**Copy:** How much the ground heats up and cools down between day and night. Big swings stress materials, seals and electronics. The Moon's equator swings by roughly 300 °C, which no place on Earth comes close to. Mars swings less, and some Earth deserts get closer to it.

### F3 · Regolith thermophysics (thermal inertia class)
**Label:** Ground type (heat retention)
**Copy:** How quickly the surface heats and cools, which tells us whether the ground is fine dust, sand, gravel or solid rock. That matters for safe landing, for driving rovers and for digging. Earth uses a different measurement method, so we compare each site's *rank* within its own world, not raw numbers.

### F4 · Slope / landability
**Label:** Flat, landable ground
**Copy:** The share of the area flat enough for a lander to set down safely. NASA's lunar lander requirement is a tilt of 8° or less, with 5° as the goal. Steep, broken ground is risky for landers, rovers and walking astronauts.

### F5 · Local relief
**Label:** Hills and relief
**Copy:** How much the ground rises and falls nearby, such as ridges, crater rims and valleys. Relief shapes shadows, line of sight for radios, and how hard a site is to traverse. We compare relief rather than altitude, because "sea level" means nothing on the Moon or Mars.

### F6 · Lithology
**Label:** Rock type
**Copy:** The main kind of rock at the surface, for example dark volcanic basalt or the pale anorthosite of the lunar highlands. Matching rock type lets geologists rehearse sampling and lets engineers test drills and tools on realistic material. Lunar-highland rock is rare on Earth, so expect fewer good matches here for Moon targets.

### F7 · Aridity
**Label:** Dryness
**Copy:** How little water the land receives compared with how much the air could evaporate. The Moon and Mars are bone-dry at the surface, so the driest deserts on Earth, such as the Atacama, are the best stand-ins. Dryness matters for testing life-detection instruments and for how soil behaves.

### F8 · Barrenness
**Label:** Bare ground (no plants)
**Copy:** How much of the land is bare rock, sand, snow or ice instead of plants, crops or buildings. A lifeless-looking surface is closer to the Moon and Mars, and it keeps vegetation from confusing tests of cameras, spectrometers and robots.

### F9 · Ground ice / cold traps
**Label:** Frozen ground / ice
**Copy:** Whether water ice is likely just below the surface, as in permanently frozen ground on Earth or shadowed "cold traps" on the Moon. Ice is the key resource for drinking water, oxygen and rocket fuel. Arctic and Antarctic permafrost lets teams practise finding and extracting it.

### F10 · Illumination regime
**Label:** Sun angle & daylight pattern
**Copy:** How high the Sun climbs and how daylight changes through the year. At the Moon's south pole the Sun skims the horizon, casting very long shadows that challenge solar power and camera vision. High-latitude Earth sites get a similar low Sun in summer, though never the airless, black-sky contrast.

### F11 · Atmospheric shielding (radiation + air pressure) — tie-breaker only
**Label:** Air & radiation shielding (tie-breaker)
**Copy:** How much air and magnetic field sit overhead to block cosmic radiation. The Moon has neither, and Mars has very thin air. Their surfaces receive roughly 100–200 times the natural radiation dose on Earth, so no Earth site truly matches. This factor only breaks ties between places that already score well, and the real gap appears in the Fidelity Gap panel.

### F12 · Surface UV — tie-breaker only
**Label:** UV exposure (tie-breaker)
**Copy:** How strong the Sun's ultraviolet light is at the ground. The Moon gets it unfiltered and Mars gets far more than Earth because its thin air has almost no ozone. High, dry Earth sites get closer but never match, so this factor only breaks ties.

### F13 · Geomorphic flags
**Label:** Landforms (tie-breaker)
**Copy:** Whether the site has telltale landforms shared with the target: impact craters, lava tubes and caves, or frozen-ground patterns. These features let crews rehearse specific tasks, such as exploring a cave or sampling crater rocks, and the challenge names caves explicitly.

## Not scored (shown on target cards and in the Fidelity Gap panel)

- **Gravity:** The Moon has about one-sixth of Earth's gravity and Mars about three-eighths. No place on Earth can change gravity, so we point to facilities that can, such as parabolic flights, drop towers and astronaut offload rigs.
- **Vacuum:** The Moon has essentially no air. Only vacuum chambers reproduce that.
- **Earth visibility (Moon only):** Whether Earth is in the sky for direct radio contact. It describes the Moon site itself, so we never score Earth places on it.
- **Logistics:** Travel time and the nearest airport. These are used to suggest practical site sets, not to measure similarity.
